# Default writing style

These are defaults, not requirements. The skill asks the deck owner whether to use them or their own rules; if they supply their own, use theirs.

## Voice

- Plain, understated British English. Matter-of-fact sentences over punchy fragments.
- Speaker notes are spoken English: contractions, short sentences, the way the presenter would actually say it.
- Calibrate to the audience. For peers and colleagues, drop anything that reads as performing at them.

## Patterns to avoid (they read as machine-written)

- Em-dash chains. Prefer full stops, commas, or colons; two short sentences beat one spliced one.
- The "not X, it's Y" reversal template ("It's not about speed. It's about trust."). At most one per deck, placed deliberately.
- Arrow chains in prose ("run → assess → improve"). Write the words; arrows belong in diagrams.
- Bold scattered mid-sentence. Bold key terms only, ideally at the start of a bullet.
- Clever or parallel-numbered headlines ("Three Experiments, Three Findings"). Use plain descriptions of what the slide contains.
- Drama adverbs and intensifiers: dramatically, genuinely, significantly, "changed everything". State the specific fact instead.
- Triad pile-ups ("from code, to process, to discovery") when they cluster.

## Truthfulness of narrative

- Never impose chronology, stages, numbered sequences, or intentionality the author has not stated. If events were parallel or organic, present them as a set. Where the author gives an explicit order, use exactly that and no more.
- Frame lessons as hindsight when they were hindsight.

## Slides vs notes

- No on-slide discussion prompts, rhetorical questions, or presenter theatre ("Pause here", "Let this sink in") on slides.
- Delivery cues and optional room-questions live in the speaker notes, phrased as brief private reminders.
